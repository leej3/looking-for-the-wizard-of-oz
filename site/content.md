| 1 | If a data center is built near your home, does your electricity bill go up? Show of hands. Who thinks it goes up? Who thinks it goes down? I'd want to hear from the people who kept their hands down, too. The honest answer is that it depends. Suppose the utility has to build a new line, and the company ends up using half the power it asked for. Who agreed to pay for the other half? That's a decision people make. In Ohio, regulators actually wrote a rule requiring large data centers served by one utility to pay for some of the capacity they reserved, even if they used less. | What happens to your electric bill? | - | Let both groups raise their hands. Give the people who abstain a moment before answering. The rule applies to covered AEP Ohio customers. Report source 9. |
| 2 | I follow AI closely, so after the midterms I was curious how much the fight over data centers had figured in the blue wave. In the AP Voter Poll, about three-quarters of voters opposed having a new data center in their community. About a quarter said AI data centers were crucial to their vote for Congress. I could imagine opposing a project down the road and choosing my representative on health care. Still, a quarter is a lot of people. What I wanted to know next was whether electing people who campaigned on this issue would actually change what gets built. | About 3 in 4 opposed a local data center. About 1 in 4 called the issue crucial to their vote. | - | Pause between the findings. Both are approximate national estimates from separate AP Voter Poll questions. Report source 2. |
| 3 | How unwelcome are these buildings? Gallup asked Americans about two kinds of facilities in their area. Seventy-one percent opposed an AI data center. Fifty-three percent opposed a nuclear power plant. That surprised me. People who opposed data centers talked about the environment. People who favored them talked about jobs. And in conversations I've had, especially with younger people, the concern sometimes goes well beyond what gets built down the road. What kind of work will there be for me? If a machine can do the thing I've spent years learning, what happens to that part of my life? I can hear those concerns without knowing which one drove somebody's vote. | AI data center: 71% opposed Nuclear plant: 53% opposed | - | March 2026 Gallup survey of U.S. adults. Parallel local-construction questions, not a forced choice between facilities. The closing questions are your reflections, not poll quotes. Report source 6. |
| 4 | Take Virginia. Count the power reported for operating data centers and you get about nine gigawatts. Add the buildings under construction and the ones still on paper and the figure reaches about forty-one. That's roughly a tenth of the reported U.S. total on that measure. There's an important difference between an operating campus and a proposal somebody can still vote down. For a neighbor that proposal is a question about land, noise, water and jobs. For the country it's also a question about how much computing capacity we want and where we can put it. Both are reasonable ways of looking at the same building. | Virginia: 9 GW operational; 41 GW including construction and plans | - | Say “reported” and “about.” DCMap, Oct. 12 snapshot: 9.0 GW operational, 8.3 under construction, 23.3 planned. Reported power mixes measures and is incomplete. It is not metered consumption. Report source 33. |
| 5 | In Oregon, Ohio, two retirees, Jim and Laura Davis, helped gather signatures so residents could vote on a restriction. The developer gave a quarter of a million dollars to defeat it. Four union groups gave another eighty-five thousand dollars to a separate committee on the same side. I can understand wanting that construction work. I can also understand wanting a vote on what goes up next door. About six in ten voters approved the restriction. Two days later the developer said the project wouldn't go ahead as proposed. So here's one place where we know what voters decided and what it changed. | Oregon, Ohio voted to limit new data centers over 25 MW | - | Speak conversationally. Contributions are amounts raised, not proof of how anyone voted. Report sources 17–19. |
| 6 | Now suppose the customer still needs that computing power. Where do they go? In Prince William County, Virginia, QTS lost a chance to expand when the Digital Gateway proposal fell through. QTS also had a campus in the same county. Imagine you're the customer. You can look for another town willing to build. Or you can call somebody who already controls space and ask what they'll charge. QTS could get that call. I don't know whether it did, or whether it had room. What interests me is that the very same company could lose the right to build and suddenly have something more valuable down the road. | Who already owns the capacity? | - | Pause after the customer's question. The canceled development and existing campus are documented; a gain on that campus is conditional. Report sources 29–31. |
| 7 | This matters to me because building these facilities takes an enormous amount of capital. As AI becomes part of more jobs, the terms on which workers and businesses can use those machines will matter. If my town says no and the project moves, another community gets the work and the tax revenue, along with the noise and the demand on its infrastructure. If lots of towns say no and customers keep calling, the people who got there first could have more say over the price and who gets access. Either way, somebody has to live with the decision. Fairness means asking whether those people had a voice and what they get in return. | If demand holds, who gets the next call? | - | Give the relocation and scarcity possibilities equal space. Both depend on what customers actually do. |
| 8 | So if a data center is proposed near me, I'd still want an answer about my electric bill. I'd want to see the terms. But I'd also want to know what happens if we turn it down. Does the work move? Does it disappear? Or does the company that owns the last data center get a customer it might otherwise have lost? I started out wondering whether data centers helped decide an election. What I'm more curious about now is what that election will actually decide. | What will the election actually decide? | - | Echo the opening. Leave a beat after the last sentence. |
