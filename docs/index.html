Henry Littlefield’s 1964 interpretation connects Dorothy’s silver shoes and the yellow brick road to the Populist campaign for silver coinage. Falling wheat prices meant a farmer had to sell more wheat to repay a fixed debt. Populists hoped expanding the money supply would lift prices. Farmers and creditors therefore had different interests in the same monetary policy.

The illustration depicts Littlefield’s reading. The parallel for data centers is the distribution of costs and benefits among people affected by the same decision.

Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh estimates that if U.S. AI infrastructure reaches 188 GW by 2032, investment would average 3.2% of GDP per year over 2025–2032, about one-third more relative to the size of the economy. By mid-2022, Virginia had more hyperscale data center capacity than all of Europe. Synergy compared the power available to IT equipment across 19 large cloud and internet service providers. Amazon, Microsoft and Google already operated infrastructure in Northern Virginia.

DCMap’s October 2026 Virginia inventory reports 9.0 GW at operating sites, 8.3 GW under construction and 23.3 GW planned. The rounded 41 GW total includes proposals whose completion is uncertain. The figures describe reported capacity rather than metered electricity consumption. Published figures vary in scope, and some sites lack power data.

Synergy and DCMap cover different facilities and use different power measures. Their figures establish Virginia’s importance before the generative AI boom and the scale of its current infrastructure. They do not establish how much of the 9 GW existed in 2022 or how much was subsequently built for AI.

Gallup’s April 2026 panel asked people to explain in their own words why they favored or opposed a local data center. Among supporters, 55% mentioned jobs and 13% mentioned tax revenue or investment. Among opponents, 50% cited resource use, including 18% who mentioned water and 18% who mentioned power. Noise accounted for 9% of opponents’ responses and falls within the broader pollution category.

Each percentage uses supporters or opponents as its denominator. A respondent could mention several reasons. The April follow-up was separate from the March survey that measured overall support.

Pew asked U.S. adults how data centers affect five aspects of local life. From January to August, the share answering “mostly bad” rose from 30% to 49% for nearby residents’ quality of life, 39% to 54% for the environment, 38% to 50% for home energy costs, 15% to 24% for local jobs and 12% to 21% for local tax revenue. Among rural adults, the corresponding shares rose from 28% to 45% for quality of life, 32% to 50% for the environment and 32% to 46% for energy costs. The share of all adults who had never heard of data centers fell from 25% to 12%. These group-level changes do not identify which individuals changed their views.

Pew reports full-sample 95% margins of ±1.4 percentage points in January (n = 8,512) and ±1.3 in July–August (n = 10,548). Assuming independent waves, SE(change) = √[(1.4/1.96)² + (1.3/1.96)²] ≈ 0.975 points. The approximate 95% half-width is 1.96 × SE ≈ 1.91 points. The intervals for the increases are 17.1–20.9 points for quality of life, 13.1–16.9 for the environment, 10.1–13.9 for energy costs and 7.1–10.9 for both jobs and tax revenue. Each whisker shows uncertainty in the change, positioned at its August endpoint on the absolute percentage axis. These calculations use rounded survey-wide margins. Panel overlap and item-specific design effects could alter the intervals.

A town can decline a proposal. If the developer finds another site, construction jobs and potential tax revenue move with the project. The new host also takes on demands for land and electricity. Local tax agreements affect the size of the benefit.

If enough projects stall and demand for computing holds, a company that already controls powered space may face fewer competing sites when negotiating a lease. An existing fixed-price lease can delay any gain until renewal. This possible benefit does not establish that an owner backed efforts to stop construction.

Oregon’s vote settled the fate of one proposal. The broader economic outcome depends on what happens elsewhere. If another community hosts the project, some of its benefits and costs move there. If new construction slows widely while demand remains strong, companies that already own operating sites may gain bargaining power when leases renew. An owner can benefit from that outcome regardless of who organized the opposition.
